## Part A: Failure points shared by all three ideas

These apply equally to #4, #5 and #6. They are listed once here and then referred to as A1–A7.

**A1. The founder may not be able to get paid at all.** Dodo's accepted-countries list was cut off again before "N", so Nepal's merchant status is still unconfirmed. Dodo's Nepal landing page is about *accepting payments from* Nepali customers, which is a different thing. "Nepal is one of 220+ countries and regions where Dodo Payments enables merchants to accept payments from customers worldwide" [SE] dodopayments.com/payments-in/nepal (date unknown). Dodo's own feedback board has an open request: "Developers and founders in under-served countries are blocked at onboarding because Dodo doesn't recognize their country or their accepted forms of ID" [SE] feedback.dodopayments.com (date unknown). [I] Until this is confirmed in writing, every other point below is secondary.

**A2. Solo operators mostly don't use relationship software at all.** "Just 1 in 10 (11% of) sole proprietors use CRM software, as do 1 in 5 (20% of) SMBs with 1-9 employees". Contact-management use was 8% among sole proprietors. [SE] marketingcharts.com/cross-media-and-traditional/local-and-small-biz-226721, Act! survey of 1,146 US SMB owners (May 2022, old). [I] All three ideas are, at heart, a light CRM with reminders. The buyer is in the segment that adopts this kind of tool least. "They're under-served" and "they don't want it" fit the same data equally well.

**A3. Churn among micro-businesses is probably worse than the 5–7% planned in 03 R3.** One source gives micro-businesses (<10 employees) "about 8.9% monthly churn (69.1% annually) with average contracts of 4.6 months" [SE] userjot.com/blog/saas-churn-rate-benchmarks (2025, vendor blog; a second site repeats the same figures, so they may share one dataset). Another says "43% of all SMB customer losses occurring within the first quarter post-purchase" [SE] same result set, source page unclear. [I] At 8.9% a month the average lifetime is about 11 months, not 17. At $12 a month less about $1.05 in fees, lifetime gross falls from about $186 to about $121, and that leaves very little room for any acquisition cost.

**A4. No study found shows that simply *asking* produces referrals.** The field experiments I found test *how* to ask, not *whether* to ask:
- A prosocial wording raised referrals in a 100,000-customer trial [SE] ideas.repec.org/a/inm/orisre/v31y2020i1p16-36.html (Jung et al., *Information Systems Research* 2020, old).
- "Referral coupons are, on average, less effective than regular coupons in generating sales from primary recipients", and "don't ask for referrals from all of your customers" [SE] knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/impact-referral-coupons/ (Iyengar, interview; old).
- [I] Every survey in 01 shows that referrals *happen*. None shows that a *prompted ask* adds to them, as opposed to taking credit for referrals that would have happened anyway. That gap is the core of the product claim for all three ideas.

**A5. The same idea has failed before.** Spreadable was a referral tool for small businesses, built by Grasshopper. It targeted "difficulty asking satisfied customers for referrals" and "difficulty quantifying referral impact". It launched in 2010 and "shut down a few months later" after "just over $550,000" was put into it [SE] grasshopper.com/blog/spreadable; mixergy.com/?p=15649 (old). Ambassador, a referral-marketing SaaS, reached end of life in September 2021, "reason… not publicly known" [SE] referralrock.com/rms/?p=9672 (old). A student critique of Spreadable argued that "referral emails sent from a service, rather than from the business owner, tend to be ignored" [SE] blogs.ubc.ca/rchan07/?p=70 (old, opinion). [I] The deep-link design (the user sends from their own phone) answers that critique. But Spreadable had more money and a parent company with distribution, and it still failed. These are old, B2B-flavoured precedents, so their weight is moderate.

**A6. Users won't be able to see what the tool earned them.** Most referrals happen in conversation, offline. The tool only knows what the user types in. Of 526 small firms surveyed, 22% had "no tracking at all" of customer sources and 34% used only "informal observation" [SE] pymnts.com/?p=4208042 (2026). An older slide deck says "40% of businesses don't track or measure referrals at all" [SE] slideshare.net/…/making-referrals-happen (undated, unverified). [I] If more than half of small firms don't record where customers come from, the tool's "you earned $X from referrals" screen will be under-filled. The user then sees $12 a month going out with no proven return, and cancels. The core test ("users earn more than they pay") can't be shown to the user, so it can't keep them paying.

**A7. The home market has the lowest willingness and ability to pay, and the high-paying markets are far away.**
- Indian SaaS buyers pay "an almost 50% discount" on average [SE] decentralised.co/p/the-saas-index (date unknown). A pricing guide says "Indian customers typically pay 50-70% less than US/EU customers" [SE] upgrowth.in/saas-pricing-packaging-strategy-india-gtm (date unknown, unsourced).
- Nepal's prepaid dollar card is still "capped at USD 500 in one given year". No 2026 change was found [SE] b360nepal.com/cross-border-payments-in-nepal (undated).
- A separate headline, "NRB raises spending ceiling for Nepalis with Forex accounts" [SE] sharesansar.com (date not shown), suggests freelancers who hold forex accounts may have more room. **Unknown** how much.
- [I] South Asian users can't easily pay $10–12 in USD, and the US/UK users who can are reachable only from 7,000 km away with no budget. Every idea has to choose one of these two bad options.

## Part B: #4, the solo review-and-referral engine for local service providers ($12/mo)

1. **Google gives away the core action for free, in the channel the product relies on.** Google's official help page lets a business "create and share a link or QR code to request reviews". It suggests sending "review requests through email, WhatsApp message, or Facebook post" [SE] support.google.com/business/answer/16816815 (QR feature announced March 2025). The free WhatsApp Business app has saved quick replies ("up to 50") and chat labels [SE] learn.rasayel.io/en/books/whatsapp/whatsapp-business-app/features/ (date unknown; caps from one source). [I] A saved quick reply containing the Google review link, plus a "needs review" label, reproduces the product's main flow at no cost. What's left is a reminder, a referral ask and tracking: the weakest part of the value. Cheap and free tools also exist: a free Google Sheets add-on, "Review Requests for Small Business" [SE] workspace.google.com/marketplace/app/review_requests_for_small_business/349282806740, and EZ Review on iOS, pay-as-you-go "as low as $0.99" [SE] apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1510786571 (developer's listing).

2. **Incumbents have already shipped this, with real triggers, inside tools the pros already pay for.**
   - Jobber: Referrals is a "$29 USD per month" add-on, and the Marketing Suite (Referrals, Reviews, Campaigns) is "$79 USD per month" [SE] help.getjobber.com/hc/en-us/articles/20959257809815 (official help, date unknown). Jobber asks for a Google review "when a job closes, when a visit is completed, or when the final invoice is paid" and "won't ask them for a review again" [SE] getjobber.com/features/marketing-tools/reviews.
   - Housecall Pro: "Review requests are automatically sent when a job is completed" [SE] help.housecallpro.com/en/articles/2637765-reviews-app.
   - Fresha (beauty) reportedly includes "automated reminders, reviews, and client marketing" [SE] zoftwarehub.com/products/fresha/overview (directory; Fresha's 2025 pricing change is disputed between sources).
   - [I] Pros already using job, booking or invoice software get automatic triggers. #4 has no trigger event and depends on the user remembering. Pros using *no* software (A2: 89% of sole proprietors have no CRM) are the least likely to adopt one.

3. **The "manual, personal sending" difference is also the reason people will stop using it.** The product has no job or payment data, so the user must log each customer and tap "send" after each job. Dex's founder says personal CRMs "never worked because it was never accessible enough - it was never in the right place at the right time", and of his own spreadsheet: "I would make a note … but I never really came back to the spreadsheet" [SE] consumerstartups.substack.com/p/-let-me-know-how-i-can-be-helpful (date unknown, founder's own account). [I] A tradesperson on a ladder is a harder case than a founder at a desk. Low use leads to no visible results (A6), and that leads to churn (A3).

4. **Reachability is close to nil.** 03 already scored R = 3. Nothing found improves it. The buyers are US, UK and Australian cleaners, handymen and beauticians, who are reached through trade Facebook groups, local SEO and field-service app marketplaces. The founder has no presence, budget or local trust in any of these. In the home market, Nepali local providers would pay in NPR, which the chosen merchants of record do not cover (03 R4, idea #18 note), and Facebook was blocked nationally in September 2025 (01). [A] No channel identified in the research reaches 100 paying US local providers at near-zero cost.

5. **Price-sensitive buyers move to the cheapest option.** A NiceJob reviewer: "Not worth it … I cut my costs in half with another program that does exactly what they do" [SE] getapp.com/marketing-software/a/nicejob/reviews/ (date unknown). G2 lists "Expensive" as a NiceJob con (01). [I] The segment that finds $75 too dear will find free (Google link plus WhatsApp) good enough. Undercutting on price wins customers who leave on price.

6. **Users stop needing it once they're "full".** [I] A sole provider's capacity is fixed. Once they have enough reviews and a full calendar, more reviews and referrals add nothing they can serve, and the subscription is the first cost cut. Asking the same small customer base again for referrals also wears out. **Counter-evidence (honest):** 74% of consumers look for reviews from the last three months (01, BrightLocal 2026), so review *recency* gives a reason to keep asking. Referrals have no such recency driver.

7. **The referral reward can expose users to review-incentive breaches.** Google prohibits "offering incentives, like free or discounted goods or services, to customers in exchange for reviews" [SE] support.google.com/business/answer/16816815 summary. The FTC rule allows penalties of up to about $52,000 per violation (03 R4). [I] A product that sends a review ask and a reward-carrying referral ask to the same customer, from the same screen, invites users to merge the two ("leave a review and get 10% off"). The founder can't control what users type into their own WhatsApp. The risk falls mainly on users, but a reputation for enabling it would hurt the product.

8. **The mechanism is half-proven, and the proven half is the free half.** BrightLocal (2023, quoted second-hand) says "65% of consumers left a review in response to a business's request" [SE] info-zone.nocowboys.co.nz/?p=2002. Its 2016 survey put it at "7 out of 10" [SE] brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey-2016/ (old). [I] This supports *review* asks, which Google's free link already handles. The *referral* half, the part that is actually differentiated, has no supporting evidence (A4).

**Verdict for #4.** The most dangerous point is **#2 together with #1**: incumbents have automatic triggers, and Google plus WhatsApp is free, which leaves #4 as a reminder app with no trigger, sold to people who don't adopt reminder apps, in a market the founder can't reach. **Cheap test:** before writing any code, offer a $12/mo pre-sale with a 14-day refund to 30 US/UK solo providers who don't use Jobber or Housecall, reached through any free channel. If fewer than 3 pay, and the founder can't name a repeatable channel that produced them, kill it.

## Part C: #5, the "client garden" for freelancers with direct clients ($10/mo)

1. **The paying segment is small, and it is not the founder's own community.** 70% of freelancers "find most of their work through online marketplaces" (Payoneer 2023, 01, old). Marketplace clients can't be taken off-platform for 24 months (02). Only 11% of sole proprietors use a CRM at all (A2). [I] The users must have direct clients, a large enough past-client base to garden, and the habit of using software. Each condition shrinks the market, and Nepali marketplace freelancers, the founder's reachable network, mostly fail the first. 03 already flagged this (R3 "excludes much of the founder's home audience"). Nothing found reverses it.

2. **Personal CRM is a known graveyard, and even a funded winner grew slowly.**
   - Dex's own landscape post is titled "Personal CRM in 2024: 20+ startups, apps, and failed attempts" and says "many past attempts … have since been shut down or acquired" [SE] blog2.getdex.com/blog/personal-crm-in-2020-20-startups-apps-and-failed-attempts (2024).
   - Network Hippo, a social CRM, shut down because "there wasn't a clear way to monetize the product" and the space was "very crowded" [SE] betakit.com/social-crm-tool-network-hippo-shuts-down… (old). Etacts shut down "less than a year after launching" [SE] techcrunch.com/2010/12/21/email-contact-manager-etacts-shuts-down (old). Nudge.AI shut down [SE] strategyonline.ca/?p=185443 (date unknown).
   - Clay ended as an acquisition by Automattic (June 2025), rebranded to Mesh [SE] techcrunch.com/?p=3017988; me.sh/stories/mesh (Mar 2026). That is not a standalone business result.
   - Dex is YC S19 and venture-backed. It reported "$0 to $11K in monthly revenue in 2021" and, in March 2022, "15,000 users across 20 countries and 1,000 customers", with an LTV of $231 (Jan 2022) [SE] republic.co/dex; getdex.com/blog/celebrating-1000/ (old).
   - [I] A funded YC team with a general relationship CRM took about 2.5 years to reach 1,000 paying customers, at an LTV of about $231. A solo founder in Nepal, in a narrower freelancer niche, should expect less. The 03 claim that there is "no focused competitor" may mean the focused niche doesn't pay, not that it's open.

3. **The biggest incumbent shipped this exact feature in August 2025.** HoneyBook AI "will find leads you haven't heard from in a while and automatically draft email replies to get back in touch". And "when HoneyBook identifies that a project might be completed, it'll suggest you send a referral request", with "a referral link to an editable lead form that captures who referred the contact" [SE] help.honeybook.com/en/articles/11669595-use-follow-up-suggestions-to-re-engage-past-leads; help.honeybook.com/en/articles/9055403 (Aug 2025). [I] That is past-client reactivation, a timed referral ask and referral-source tracking. Those are the three core jobs of #5, inside the tool the best-paid direct-client freelancers already use for contracts and invoices. Round 2's "no product found that focuses on…" is now out of date for HoneyBook users.

4. **Free and near-free substitutes cover the rest.** Monica is "free and open-source under the AGPL-3.0 license", with hosted plans at "$90 per year, or $9 per month" [SE] getdex.com/blog/monica-review/ (Apr 2026, written by a competitor). A recurring calendar event ("check in with Client X, 6 months after launch") plus a notes app costs nothing. [I] The real job is a dozen reminders a year per freelancer, which is very little value to bill monthly.

5. **Usage is too infrequent for a subscription, and users churn from both ends.** [I] A freelancer with 15–40 past direct clients may have 2–6 sensible check-ins a month. When they're busy, they don't need reactivation. When they're short of work, $10 a month is the first cut, and the garden has already done its few asks. Personal-CRM churn is **unknown**, but micro-business benchmarks (A3) and Dex's $231 LTV (about 19 months at $12) set the ceiling.

6. **The ask itself is contested for freelancers.** freelancermap (whose 2025 study 03 uses as *support* for referrals) says asking "can feel like begging and will often not get you the necessary results", and that referrals "should not be asked for, but rather earned" [SE] freelancermap.com/freelancer-tips/7816-how-to-get-referrals-by-earning-them-instead-of-asking (opinion). No study measures ask against no-ask (A4). [I] 03 cites freelancermap's "62% from passive sources such as referrals" as demand evidence. The same source argues that the product's *mechanism* (prompted asks) is the wrong one.

7. **Users can't see what it earned them.** [I] When a past client comes back, it looks like the client's own decision, and the freelancer gives the credit to their own work, not to the reminder. The tool can't prove that it caused the repeat project (A6). Users who can't see a return cancel.

8. **Price compared with income in the founder's region.** [I] For the Nepal average freelancer income of $4,723 a year (01), $120 a year is 2.5% of income, before the $500 card cap (A7). Price-parity norms suggest about $5. At that price Dodo's fixed $0.40 fee takes 14–16% (03 R3).

**Verdict for #5.** The most dangerous point is **#3 combined with #2**: HoneyBook has shipped reactivation and referral asks to the paying end of the segment, and the history of personal CRMs shows that even funded teams struggle to get people to pay for relationship reminders. **Cheap test:** interview 15 freelancers who earn mostly from *direct* clients (not marketplaces). Count how many (a) have 15 or more past clients, (b) don't already use HoneyBook, Dubsado or Dex, and (c) say they'd pay $10/mo after seeing a clickable mock-up. If fewer than 5 of 15 meet all three, kill it.

## Part D: #6, the independent-tutor growth kit ($12/mo)

1. **The tutors who can pay don't need it, and the ones who need it have no one to ask.** One tutor got to "about 12-13 people i tutor each week" with "no advertising at all", and another "never had to advertise" after the first students [SE] physicsforums.com/threads/tutoring-math-tips-advice-for-16-year-olds.269751/; forums.eslcafe.com/job/viewtopic.php?p=1087680 (old forum anecdotes). The other side: "once I get going then word of mouth will work, but I just can't seem to get the initial clients to start word of mouth" [SE] city-data.com/forum/teaching/2063154… (old). [I] Referral and review tools amplify an existing parent base. Full tutors don't need more students, and empty tutors have no parents to ask. The buyer is the narrow middle group: established, not full, and willing to pay for software. Its size is **unknown**.

2. **Hours are capped, so users get "full" and then churn, and the business is seasonal on top of that.** [I] A tutor's sellable hours are fixed. Once full, the referral, review and waitlist features have nothing left to grow. The waitlist is useful exactly when nothing else in the kit is. Seasonality adds more churn: "summer break can cut tutoring revenue" [SE] blog.teachworks.com/2025/05/4-tips-for-boosting-your-summer-tutoring-revenue/ (May 2025). One tutor reports a peak of "3-5 students per week" falling to "0-3" in summer [SE] careers.mkausa.org/side-hustle/stories/ata-tutoring (date unknown, single anecdote). Tutor.com: "peak usage is between September and mid-June" [SE] tutor.com/appcentral/ac-gs-381. [I] Expect cancellations every summer and when tutors become full, on top of the A3 baseline.

3. **Incumbents and platforms already hold the tutor's workflow, and adding a feature is cheap for them.**
   - TutorBird is now listed at "$16.95/month for one user", 4.8/5 from about 265–268 reviews. It already has parent booking ("the ability for parents to book slots") and "automated lesson reminders" [SE] capterra.ie / capterra.co.uk listings for TutorBird (date unknown; 03 cited $14.95, so the price has risen or sources differ).
   - [I] TutorBird holds the parent contacts, lesson history and invoices: everything needed to time a referral ask or write a progress note. Adding "send a review/referral request" is a small feature for them. For a tutor, #6 would be a second tool that duplicates the student list.
   - Wyzant already offers tutors a built-in referral programme: "When a new student creates a Wyzant account and attends lessons with the referring tutor, that tutor receives 100% of their rate" [SE] support.wyzant.com/hc/en-us/articles/115002724723 (official help). [I] For US Wyzant tutors, the platform already provides referrals at 0% commission plus payment protection, scheduling and reviews, for free.
   - In South Asia, Wise (wise.live) markets tutor management with "branded Android and iOS apps", and Capterra shows a "$0 starting price" (conflicting with "$20 / license") [SE] capterra.in/software/1033151/wise. Teachmint is listed "starting $5 per user" [SE] capterra.in/software/163105/teachmint. These are cheaper anchors in the founder's own region.

4. **The pitch to the most motivated users is the one the product must refuse.** The sharpest pain in the evidence is marketplace commission and lock-in (Wyzant's 25%, Preply's 18–33%; 01). But marketplaces forbid circumvention. Preply: "exchanging contact details or sharing external links before booking a trial lesson is prohibited" [SE] help.preply.com/es/articles/4179497 (official, student-facing). MyTutor: "we'll close their account immediately" if tutors look like taking work off-platform [SE] help.mytutor.co.uk/en/articles/8286635-disintermediation-policy-for-tutors. [I] The product must exclude marketplace-sourced students (02 note). That removes the main emotional hook ("escape the 25%") and leaves only tutors who already have independent parents. Tutors will also inevitably try to use it on marketplace students, which puts *their* accounts at risk and makes the product look complicit.

5. **The mechanism evidence is the weakest of the three.** 03 R1 admits: "No tutor survey with a percentage was found." The only quantified case is one vendor anecdote ("60% … via referrals after offering 20% discounts", wise.live, 03). Iyengar's field work finds referral coupons "less effective than regular coupons" (A4). [I] The tutor-controlled reward (a free lesson for a referral) is exactly a referral coupon. Parent networks are also local and small. The size of a parent's referral pool is **unknown**.

6. **The home market can't pay in the product's currency, and the paying markets are unreachable.** Nepali home tutors earn "NPR 5,000 to NPR 12,000 per month per student" as beginners, and 15,000–30,000 when experienced [SE] necojobs.com.np/blogs/career-tips/home-tuition-jobs-in-nepal (date unknown, jobs blog). [A] At roughly NPR 135–140 per USD (rate not checked), one student is worth about $37–220 a month, so the "one student pays for five months" maths (03 R3) can work on paper. But these tutors earn and spend in NPR, face the $500 card cap (A7), and Dodo and Paddle bill in foreign currency. Billing in NPR through Khalti or eSewa needs a registered business (03, #18). [I] The users who might pay can't easily pay through the chosen merchant of record. The users who can pay easily (US/UK tutors) are reached only through Wyzant/Preply communities, TutorBird-style SEO or tutor podcasts, none of which the founder has a presence in.

7. **Monthly progress notes are a chore, and admin tools' own users don't ask for growth features.** [I] Writing a monthly note per student (even AI-drafted) is new unpaid work on top of 10–20 students. The TutorBird complaints found are about calendar sync, niche settings and "a place specifically for parents to store report cards" [SE] capterra.co.uk/reviews/181623/tutorbird. They ask for admin features, not acquisition features. That is weak evidence that growth features are not top of mind for tutors who already pay for software.

8. **Data about minors raises the compliance bar.** [I] The product stores parents' contacts and progress notes about children. For EU/UK users this is personal data about minors under GDPR, beyond the general data-processor duties noted in 03 R4. Nepal's Individual Privacy Act requirements are still **unknown** (03). [A] This is not a blocker, but it adds legal cost and risk for one person.

9. **The tutoring precedents are weak but negative.** Tutorspree (YC W11, a marketplace) shut down because it "would not scale in a way that met its goals" [SE] venturebeat.com/technology/airbnb-for-tutoring-startup-tutorspree-shuts-down (old). FEV Tutor stopped operating in January 2025 after "weaker sales and a shift in capital commitments from its lender" [SE] the74million.org/article/major-virtual-tutoring-provider-shuts-down… (2025). [I] These are marketplaces and school-contract businesses, not tutor tools, so they show only that tutoring is a hard market, not that #6 specifically fails.

**Verdict for #6.** The most dangerous point is **#1 combined with #2**: the paying user (an established tutor) becomes "full" and then churns, the needy user (a new tutor) has no parents to ask, and the evidence that prompted parent referrals add students is a single vendor anecdote. **Cheap test:** find 20 independent tutors with 5 or more non-marketplace students who are *not* full. Have them send a scripted parent referral ask by hand from WhatsApp, at no charge and with no software, for 6 weeks. Count the new enquiries and enrolments the asks produced. If fewer than about 1 in 4 tutors gains a student, the mechanism the product depends on isn't there. Also record how many of the 20 were hard to find. That count is the real reachability data.

## Part E: Overall conclusion

- **The shared fatal risk.** All three ideas depend on two things that remain unproven:
  - **A1:** whether the founder can get paid through Dodo or Paddle from Nepal at all.
  - **A4:** whether *prompting* asks produces revenue the user can see and attribute (A6).
- **Each idea's distinctive attack.** Nothing found in this round raises any score.
  - **#4:** Google, WhatsApp and field-service suites already cover it.
  - **#5:** HoneyBook shipped its core in August 2025, and personal CRMs have a poor commercial record.
  - **#6:** the full-or-empty paradox, plus a seasonal churn floor.
- **Scores.** #4 and #5 should lose points on Differentiation. #6's Retention score of 6 looks optimistic given seasonality and capacity limits.
- **What the reviewer did not find (stated so it is not mistaken for absence).** No failed or pivoted startup specifically in "review/referral tool for *solo* local providers" or "tutor marketing tool". No survey of tutors' willingness to pay for marketing software. No churn data for personal CRMs or tutor tools. These remain **unknown**, not favourable.
